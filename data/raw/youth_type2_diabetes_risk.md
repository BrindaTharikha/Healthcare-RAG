## Weight as a risk factor
About one-third of American youth have overweight, which doesn't automatically mean a child is unhealthy, but it does increase the risk of insulin resistance, a major risk factor for type 2 diabetes in both kids and adults. High blood pressure and high cholesterol, both linked to overweight, can further increase this risk. Since young kids and teens are still growing, the goal for a child with overweight is typically to slow weight gain while still supporting healthy growth and development, best discussed with a doctor for specific dietary guidance.

## Physical activity as a risk factor
Physical inactivity increases the risk of type 2 diabetes, while regular activity helps the body use insulin more effectively and lowers the risk of overweight. Most kids and teens don't get the recommended amount of daily physical activity.

## Age and hormonal factors
Type 2 diabetes in children is usually diagnosed in the early teen years, in part because hormonal changes during puberty make it harder for the body to use insulin effectively. This effect is especially pronounced in girls, who are more likely than boys to develop type 2 diabetes during this period.

## Additional risk factors and testing
Other risk factors for type 2 diabetes in youth include having a family member with type 2 diabetes, being born to a parent who had gestational diabetes, being Hispanic/Latino, Black, Native American/Alaska Native, Asian American, or Pacific Islander, and having overweight, obesity, high blood pressure, or high cholesterol. If a child has two or more of these risk factors, parents should talk to a doctor about blood sugar testing, which typically begins at age 10 or at the onset of puberty, whichever comes first, and is repeated every 3 years.

## Family-style prevention: mealtime changes
Making healthy changes as a family tends to be more successful than individual effort, since shared habits are easier to sustain. Helpful mealtime changes include drinking more water and fewer sugary drinks, eating more fruits and vegetables, making healthier swaps (such as whole grains and lean meats), involving kids in meal preparation, eating together at the table rather than in front of a screen, teaching kids to read food labels, avoiding pressure to finish every plate, and using praise rather than food as a reward.

## Family-style prevention: physical activity
A general goal is 60 minutes of physical activity a day for children, which doesn't need to happen all at once. Making activity enjoyable, trying new activities, asking kids what they prefer, limiting screen time, planning active outings like walking or biking, and turning chores into active games can all help build sustainable habits.
