## Overview: complications are decreasing but related
Rates of several major diabetes complications have decreased among U.S. adults over the past 20 years, with the greatest declines in heart attack and stroke, two leading causes of death. Diabetes complications often share risk factors and can worsen one another. For example, high blood pressure (common in people with diabetes) can worsen eye and kidney disease, and diabetes tends to lower "good" (HDL) cholesterol while raising triglycerides and "bad" (LDL) cholesterol, increasing heart disease and stroke risk. Smoking doubles the risk of heart disease in people with diabetes.

## Heart disease and stroke
People with diabetes are twice as likely to have heart disease or a stroke compared to people without diabetes, and may develop it more severely and at a younger age.

## Eye problems
Diabetes can cause damage to blood vessels in the retina (diabetic retinopathy), clouding of the eye's lens (cataracts), and increased fluid pressure in the eye (glaucoma).

## Kidney disease
High blood sugar levels can damage the kidneys, leading to chronic kidney disease (CKD); about 1 in 3 adults with diabetes has CKD, and if untreated, CKD can progress to kidney failure.

## Nerve damage and amputation
Nerve damage (neuropathy) is a common complication causing numbness and pain, most often in the feet and legs, and can also affect digestion, blood vessels, and the heart. Damage to blood vessels and nerves in the feet can lead to serious, hard-to-treat infections, sometimes requiring amputation to stop the spread of infection.

## Other related complications
Gum disease can contribute to tooth loss and higher blood sugar (and can also increase the risk of developing type 2 diabetes). Diabetes increases the risk of depression, with that risk growing as more diabetes-related health problems develop. Gestational diabetes can also cause serious complications for both mothers and babies. Complications typically develop gradually without symptoms, which is why regular doctor appointments matter even when feeling well.

## Prevention toolkit: managing the ABCs
A healthy lifestyle is the foundation for preventing or delaying complications: following a healthy eating plan, getting at least 150 minutes of physical activity per week, losing weight if needed, and taking medications as instructed. "Managing the ABCs" means getting a regular A1C test, keeping blood pressure below 140/90 mm Hg, controlling cholesterol, and not smoking. Keeping regular appointments with a health care team supports all of these efforts.

## Staying supported through setbacks
Diabetes management varies by individual, and some people experience complications despite consistent effort. Feeling discouraged can sometimes lead to unhealthy habits, such as skipping blood sugar monitoring or doctor appointments. A health care team can help re-set goals, track progress, and offer new strategies, and support groups (in person or online) can connect people with others facing similar challenges.
