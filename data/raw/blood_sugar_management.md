## Checking blood sugar
Blood sugar can be checked using a blood sugar meter (glucometer), which measures sugar in a small blood sample, usually from a fingertip, or a continuous glucose monitor (CGM), which uses a sensor under the skin to measure blood sugar every few minutes. CGM users still need daily meter checks to confirm CGM accuracy. How often to check depends on diabetes type and medication; typical times include first thing in the morning, before meals, two hours after meals, and at bedtime. People with type 1 diabetes, those with type 2 diabetes who take insulin, or those who often experience low blood sugar may need to check more frequently, including around physical activity.

## Blood sugar targets
Typical blood sugar targets are 80 to 130 mg/dL before a meal, and less than 180 mg/dL two hours after the start of a meal. Targets can vary based on age, other health conditions, and individual factors, so targets should be discussed with a health care team.

## Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia)
Blood sugar below 70 mg/dL is considered low. Common causes include missing a meal, taking too much insulin or other diabetes medicine, higher-than-usual physical activity, or drinking alcohol.

## High blood sugar (hyperglycemia) and diabetic ketoacidosis
High blood sugar can be caused by illness, stress, eating more than usual, or not taking enough insulin. If someone is sick and their blood sugar is 240 mg/dL or higher, an over-the-counter ketone test kit should be used to check for ketones. High ketones can be an early sign of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), which is a medical emergency requiring immediate treatment; a doctor should be called right away if ketones are high.

## Everyday ways to manage blood sugar
Eating a healthy diet, maintaining a healthy weight, and staying physically active all support blood sugar management. Additional strategies include tracking blood sugar patterns, eating at regular times without skipping meals, choosing foods lower in calories, saturated fat, sugar, and salt, limiting alcohol (no more than 2 drinks a day for men, 1 for women), choosing fruit for a sweet treat, and controlling portion sizes.

## Carbohydrates and blood sugar
Carbohydrates raise blood sugar more than proteins or fats do, but people with diabetes can still eat carbs; the appropriate amount depends on individual factors like age, weight, and activity level. Counting carbohydrates is a useful tool for managing blood sugar, and a health care team can help determine appropriate carb goals.
