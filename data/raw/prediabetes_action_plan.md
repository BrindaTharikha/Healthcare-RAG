## Finding your motivation
Before starting a lifestyle change, it helps to identify a clear personal motivation, for example, wanting to be more active to keep up with grandchildren. Reflecting on this "why" can help someone stay on track when challenges arise. The next steps involve assessing current habits around nutrition and physical activity, then building a specific, realistic action plan with a clear cue for when to use the new routine.

## Understanding the A1C test
The A1C (hemoglobin A1C) test is a simple blood test that measures average blood sugar levels over the past 3 months, commonly used to diagnose prediabetes and diabetes. A normal A1C is below 5.7%, prediabetes falls between 5.7% and 6.4%, and diabetes is 6.5% or above. Within the prediabetes range, a higher A1C indicates greater risk of developing type 2 diabetes.

## Setting a weight loss goal
For someone who is overweight with prediabetes, losing just 5% of body weight can help reverse prediabetes, roughly 10 to 14 pounds for a 200-pound person. A weight loss goal can be calculated by taking 5% of current body weight and subtracting it from the starting weight to determine a target.

## Building a nutrition plan
A sustainable nutrition plan should be based on healthy eating and be realistic enough to maintain long-term, rather than following a restrictive or trendy diet. Helpful choices include non-starchy vegetables, fruits, lean protein, whole grains, and water, while processed foods, trans fats, sugary drinks, and alcohol should be limited. Portion control (such as the plate method), reading nutrition labels, and eating a variety of foods across food groups all support healthy eating. Cutting calories can help with weight loss, but cutting too far can make a plan hard to sustain.

## Setting an activity goal
A reasonable physical activity goal is to gradually work up to at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week, such as 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week. Starting with a smaller goal (for example, 3 days a week for 20 minutes) and building up over time, with a specific goal date, can make the habit easier to sustain. Checking with a doctor about which activities are appropriate is recommended.

## Tracking progress and getting support
Tracking food, activity, and weight, through an app, journal, or spreadsheet, is associated with higher success in reaching health goals. Sharing goals with friends or family, or finding a walking partner or accountability buddy, can provide helpful support. A health care provider can also help adjust a plan that isn't working and may refer someone to a specialist, such as a registered dietitian, if needed.
