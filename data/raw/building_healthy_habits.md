## Make a plan with SMART goals
Building a healthy habit starts with a clear plan. Define a personal "why" for the change and keep it visible as a reminder during challenges. Choose a specific goal using the SMART framework: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound. For example, instead of "I'll eat more vegetables," a SMART goal would be "I'll eat a side salad with dinner on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays."

## Be accountable
Tracking a new habit, through an app, food diary, notebook, or spreadsheet, makes it more likely to succeed, since it helps identify what's working and what isn't. Social accountability also helps: a walking partner, gym buddy, or supportive friends and family can help someone stay on track. For people with diabetes or prediabetes, structured programs like Diabetes Self-Management Education and Support (DSMES) or the National Diabetes Prevention Program (National DPP) can provide additional accountability and support.

## Recognize success
Celebrating progress with rewards that support health goals, like time for a favorite hobby or relaxation, helps sustain motivation. Results may not appear immediately, and that doesn't mean a habit isn't working. It's normal for things not to go according to plan; picking back up after a setback is itself a success worth recognizing.

## Start small
Jumping directly into a large goal, such as running a 5k, can feel overwhelming. Building confidence with small, achievable steps, for example, one day a week of 5-10 minutes of activity, and gradually increasing from there, is more sustainable. When building multiple new habits, starting with one and adding others after a few weeks of practice tends to work better than taking on everything at once.

## Act now rather than waiting
There's no need to wait for a "perfect" starting point, like the beginning of a week, month, or year, to start a new habit. Waiting for an ideal moment often becomes a way to delay starting altogether.

## Practice moderation
A sustainable habit should be something achievable for the long term, not just for a few weeks. Aiming for consistency around 90% of the time, rather than perfection, helps prevent burnout. Allowing occasional breaks or rest days, without abandoning the habit entirely, supports long-term success.

## Prepare for setbacks
Since a new habit exists alongside the rest of life's demands, it's normal for plans to be disrupted by work, family, weather, or other circumstances. Having a backup plan, such as a shorter home workout instead of a planned outdoor activity, helps maintain consistency even when circumstances change. Something is generally better than nothing.

## Adjust what isn't working
It's fine to revise a plan over time. Tracking progress helps identify what is and isn't working, so adjustments can be made based on real experience rather than assumptions. There is no single correct way to build healthier habits, finding an approach that fits an individual's preferences and lifestyle matters most.
